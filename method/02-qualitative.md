# Qualitative Research

## When to recommend a qualitative approach

Recommend qualitative research when the research problem requires exploration and understanding of meanings, experiences, processes, contexts, or perspectives that cannot be adequately reduced to predetermined variables.

Typical signals include:

- the phenomenon is insufficiently understood;
- participant meaning is central;
- the study requires open-ended inquiry;
- context is essential to interpretation;
- important variables are not yet known;
- a theory may need to emerge from data;
- the researcher needs a complex account rather than a single numerical relationship.

Qualitative research should not be selected simply because the sample is small or interviews are convenient.

## Core qualitative characteristics

A Creswell-aligned qualitative plan should normally account for:

- participant meanings;
- natural settings;
- the researcher as a key instrument;
- multiple sources of data;
- inductive and deductive analysis;
- emergent design;
- reflexivity;
- the researcher's role;
- complex interpretation.

## Design selection

### Descriptive method

Use when the goal is to remain relatively close to participants' language and the data.

Typical fit:

- organized description is the main need;
- heavy theoretical interpretation is not required;
- findings can be developed from codes into themes;
- the researcher wants a clear account before applying a deeper analytic framework.

Do not label a study "phenomenology," "grounded theory," or another analytic design when a descriptive method is sufficient.

### Narrative research

Use when the unit of interest is the life, story, or lived sequence of one or more individuals.

Typical fit:

- the research asks for stories;
- chronology is important;
- the researcher may restory events into a narrative sequence;
- participant story and researcher interpretation are central.

Avoid when the real unit of analysis is a bounded organization, a shared culture, or a general process.

### Phenomenology

Use when the study seeks the shared essence of a lived experience among people who have experienced the same phenomenon.

Typical fit:

- the phenomenon is clearly bounded conceptually;
- multiple participants have experienced it;
- lived experience is the central object of inquiry;
- the outcome is a description of the common essence of the experience.

Do not use "phenomenology" merely as a synonym for interviewing people about opinions.

### Grounded theory

Use when the study seeks to generate a general, abstract theory of a process, action, or interaction grounded in participant data.

Typical fit:

- the research question focuses on process;
- existing theory is insufficient;
- data collection and analysis are iterative;
- categories are refined and related until a theoretical explanation develops.

Do not recommend grounded theory when the study merely wants themes without theory generation.

### Ethnography

Use when the study examines shared patterns of behavior, language, actions, or meaning within a culture-sharing group.

Typical fit:

- a recognizable culture-sharing group exists;
- the natural setting matters;
- prolonged engagement is feasible;
- observation and interview data are important;
- cultural patterns rather than individual experiences are the focus.

Avoid when the researcher cannot meaningfully engage the group or setting over time.

### Case study

Use when the study requires an in-depth analysis of one or more bounded cases.

A case may be a program, event, activity, process, organization, individual, or another clearly bounded unit.

Typical fit:

- the case can be bounded by time and place or setting;
- multiple forms of data can be collected;
- the goal is depth and contextual understanding.

The AI must explicitly state **what the case is** and **how it is bounded**.

## Sampling and recruitment

Qualitative studies normally use purposeful sampling.

Identify:

- who can provide information-rich data;
- why participants or cases are relevant;
- inclusion and exclusion logic;
- site or setting;
- access and gatekeepers;
- recruitment procedure;
- whether saturation or another stopping logic is appropriate to the selected design.

Do not use quantitative representativeness language unless it genuinely applies.

## Data collection

Potential sources include interviews, observations, documents, records, audiovisual material, social-media material, and other digital materials.

The selected sources must fit the research question and design. Use data-recording protocols where appropriate, including interview and observation protocols.

## Researcher role and reflexivity

Address:

- prior experiences relevant to the topic;
- relationship to participants or site;
- cultural and historical position;
- potential influence on interpretation;
- access to the field;
- ethical sensitivity;
- steps used to reflect on and document researcher influence.

## General analytic flow

A Creswell-aligned general sequence is:

```text
1. Organize and prepare data
2. Read through the full dataset
3. Code the data
4. Develop descriptions and themes
5. Develop a story-line interpretation / connect themes
6. Apply an analytic framework when appropriate
7. Represent and interpret findings
```

The selected design may require additional specialized procedures.

Do not claim that generic code-to-theme analysis is automatically sufficient for phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography, narrative research, or case study. Where the design requires specialized analysis, consult the appropriate specialist source.

## Qualitative validation

Propose validation strategies suited to the study rather than treating validity as a checkbox.

Possible strategies discussed by Creswell & Creswell include:

- triangulation across sources or perspectives;
- member checking;
- rich, thick description;
- clarification of researcher bias through reflexivity;
- presentation of negative or discrepant evidence;
- prolonged engagement in the field;
- peer debriefing;
- external audit.

Reliability or consistency can be strengthened by clearly documenting procedures, protocols, coding decisions, and where appropriate intercoder agreement.
