# Writing Standards for International Research Articles

This document describes what distinguishes a manuscript that survives editorial screening at an international journal from one that is returned before its substance is read, and it compares the Better Research [flow](research-workflow.md) with established best practice. It is guidance for Stage 8 and for the audit at FG4. It does not replace the target journal's author guidelines, which must be verified and recorded in the project brief.

## Eleven characteristics

Each characteristic states what it means in practice and where the flow implements it.

### 1. One central contribution

The title, the abstract, the end of the introduction, and the discussion all point to the same contribution, stated in one sentence. A manuscript that "contributes five things" reads as one whose authors have not decided what they found.

*In the flow:* the contribution statement (`CTB`) is registered at Stage 4 and restated, with variation, in the four locations. The outline (Stage 8) names which paragraph carries it in each.

### 2. The introduction is an argument, not a summary of the literature

The tested model is *establishing a territory → establishing a niche → occupying the niche* (Swales 1990). The six introduction moves of the flow map onto those three. The introduction should be roughly a tenth to a seventh of the manuscript, each move one or two paragraphs, and it ends with the contribution — not with a research question left hanging. A full literature review belongs in its own section or in the theoretical grounding.

### 3. Novelty is shown, not claimed

Reviewers are sceptical of "this is the first study to…". A contribution is constructed by showing that the existing literature is incomplete, inadequate, or incommensurate with the phenomenon (Locke & Golden-Biddle 1997). Research questions that challenge the assumptions of the grand theory in a new context — problematization rather than gap-spotting (Alvesson & Sandberg 2011) — produce sharper contributions; this is what "research questions derived from the theoretical review" means in Stage 4.

*In the flow:* the terms *novelty* and *state of the art* are working vocabulary for Stages 3–4; in the manuscript they appear as a gap statement and a contribution statement. The phrase "state of the art" is common in engineering and computer science and rare in the social sciences.

### 4. Theory does work; it does not decorate

References, lists of variables, and diagrams are not theory (Sutton & Staw 1995). A theory must produce something — constructs, expectations, or a lens — and it must be used again in the discussion.

*In the flow:* the role of theory is declared at Stage 2 (lens, hypothesis generator, or sensitizing concepts) and every `THR` statement is either used by a `DIS` statement in Stage 7 or explicitly found not relevant.

### 5. Literature is synthesized, not listed

Not "according to A (2021)… according to B (2022)…" but a pattern: "studies in context X agree that…, whereas in context Y the results differ (A; B; C), possibly because…".

*In the flow:* the literature matrix carries a "position in the debate" column and its output is three to five `LIT` statements, not one summary per article.

### 6. Method is reported to the reporting standard of its design

Reviewers check the method against a checklist from the EQUATOR family or the discipline's equivalent — COREQ, SRQR, STROBE, CONSORT, PRISMA, GRAMMS, JARS. A manuscript that does not meet the relevant one is returned before its findings are read.

*In the flow:* the reporting standard is selected at Stage 5 and registered as a parameter set; FG4 verifies the completed checklist. See [reporting standards](reporting-standards.md).

### 7. Results are claims with evidence, separated from interpretation

Each result is one statement supported by one table or figure (Mensh & Kording 2017). Quantitative results report effect sizes and confidence intervals, not only p-values (Wilkinson & Task Force on Statistical Inference 1999; Wasserstein & Lazar 2016). Qualitative results present theme → sub-theme → coded participant extracts, with "power quotes" in the text and "proof quotes" in a table (Pratt 2009).

*In the flow:* the RQ ↔ finding ↔ data matrix in the brain (Stage 6) is the skeleton of the results section.

### 8. The discussion is structured

The tested order (Docherty & Smith 1999): principal findings → strengths and weaknesses of the study → relation to other studies, including differences and why → meaning and implications → unanswered questions. The flow's dialogue with literature is moves three and four.

*In the flow:* open the discussion with one paragraph of principal findings; separate theoretical from practical implications and name the actor ("for regional regulators…", not "for stakeholders").

### 9. Claims are calibrated

Causal verbs only for designs that license them; *suggests* and *indicates* for associations and interpretations, *shows* and *demonstrates* only for what was demonstrated. Tenses are consistent: past for method and results, present for established knowledge and for interpretation.

*In the flow:* FG4 includes an audit of claim verbs against the design's boundaries of inference recorded in the method decision record.

### 10. Limitations have consequences; the conclusion is not a summary

Not the boilerplate "the sample was limited" but: limitation → which inference it weakens → what was done to mitigate it → which further research follows from it. The conclusion answers the research questions, states the contribution, and stops.

*In the flow:* every `LIM` statement names the `CCL` or `IMP` it weakens; every `CCL` answers a named `RQ`.

### 11. Transparency and integrity are visible

Statements of ethics, data availability, conflicts of interest, and reflexivity (qualitative); disclosure of AI use — the ICMJE recommendations, COPE guidance, and the policies of the major publishers exclude AI systems from authorship and require disclosure of their use; references verified one by one against the source. Unverifiable citations are an increasingly common reason for rejection.

*In the flow:* the manuscript structure ends with a statements block; `research/ai-use-log.md` is its source for AI disclosure; `references.bib` holds only verified metadata.

## Figures and tables

A display item is an argument, not decoration. Reputable outlets apply the same few rules; the target journal's author guidelines override them and are recorded in the project brief.

**Each one earns its place.** A display item exists because the point cannot be made as briefly in a sentence. Three numbers belong in the text. Twenty belong in a table. A pattern, a trend, or a relationship belongs in a figure.

**Never duplicate.** The same data appears in the text, a table, or a figure — exactly one of the three. Repeating a table as a figure, or narrating every cell of a table in the text, is the most common reason an editor asks for cuts.

**Table or figure.** A table carries precise values, many variables, and readers who will look up a specific number. A figure carries shape: distribution, trend, comparison, relationship. If the reader needs the exact value, it is a table.

**Each stands alone.** The caption makes the item interpretable without the body text: what is shown, from whom or what, under which conditions, with the unit, the sample size, and every abbreviation defined. A reader who encounters the figure through a search result should understand it. Table captions conventionally sit above, figure captions below.

**Numbered in order of first mention, and every one is cited.** An uncited display item is either unnecessary or the text has a gap.

**Uncertainty is visible.** Quantitative figures show variability, not only means — intervals, distributions, or the data points themselves. A bar chart of means with no indication of spread hides the evidence it claims to show.

**Qualitative display items are specified by the tradition.** A data-structure figure showing first-order codes, second-order themes, and aggregate dimensions (Gioia, Corley & Hamilton 2013); a table of proof quotes supporting each theme, with power quotes kept in the text (Pratt 2009); a joint display placing the quantitative and qualitative strands side by side for mixed methods (Fetters, Curry & Creswell 2013). Design the joint display before the results exist, not after.

**Reproduced material needs permission.** A figure taken or adapted from another publication requires the rights holder's permission and an attribution in the caption, even when the source is open access. Record the permission with the data record.

**Accessibility and production.** Do not encode meaning by colour alone — use shape, pattern, or direct labels as well, and choose a palette that survives greyscale printing and common colour-vision deficiencies. Meet the outlet's resolution and file-format requirements; check that the text remains legible at the printed column width.

**Traceability.** Every display item cites the `DAT` or `RUN` that produced it and the statement it supports, recorded in the [manuscript outline](../templates/manuscript-outline.md). A figure whose underlying run cannot be named is not reproducible.

## Craft at paragraph and sentence level

One idea per paragraph, opened by a topic sentence. Old information before new. Subject and verb close together. The most important element in the stress position at the end of the sentence (Gopen & Swan 1990). Context → content → conclusion at the level of the paragraph, the section, and the manuscript (Mensh & Kording 2017). Terminology is consistent: one term per concept across all sections; the outline records the chosen terms.

The outline in Stage 8 goes down to the topic sentence of each paragraph. Before submission, perform a reverse outline: read only the first sentence of every paragraph; if the argument cannot be followed from those sentences, the structure is not finished.

## Comparison with established practice

| Aspect | Better Research flow | Established practice | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Six moves: phenomenon → theory → problem → gap → state of the art/novelty → RQ | CARS three moves (Swales 1990); hourglass (Bem 2004) | Consistent. End with the contribution; keep the length in check. |
| Theoretical review | One grand theory from its original source, developments within a ten-year window from Scopus Q1–Q4 and Web of Science | Functional theory (Sutton & Staw 1995); Creswell & Creswell 2023, chapter 3 | Consistent. Declare the role of theory. |
| Empirical literature review | Five-year window, Scopus, matrix | Transparent searching (PRISMA 2020); two or more databases; management reviews (Tranfield, Denyer & Smart 2003; Snyder 2019) | One correction: journal quartile is a journal-level metric, used as a priority filter, never as an eligibility or quality criterion; appraisal stays per source. Add Web of Science; allow canonical works outside the window with a recorded reason. |
| State of the art, novelty, RQ | RQ from the theoretical review and the novelty | Single contribution (Mensh & Kording 2017); problematization (Alvesson & Sandberg 2011); RQ form by approach (Creswell & Creswell 2023, chapter 7) | Consistent. Mixed methods requires an integration question. |
| Method | Approach → method → data types → data sources → collection → analysis → ethics | Creswell: approach → design → methods; APA JARS; EQUATOR checklists | Consistent in order. "Method" at step 2 is read as *design*; sampling and the quality strategy appear as explicit items because journals expect a rigor subsection. |
| Data types and sources | Follow the design; AI assists with secondary sources for triangulation | Four types of triangulation (Denzin 1978); validity strategies (Creswell & Creswell 2023) | Consistent. Name the type of triangulation; verify and log AI-assisted sources. |
| Results | Generate, then check every RQ | Results as claims plus evidence (Mensh & Kording 2017); Pratt 2009; data structure (Gioia, Corley & Hamilton 2013) | Consistent; the RQ ↔ finding ↔ data matrix covers it. |
| Discussion | Findings versus theory (relevant / not relevant), then dialogue | Structured discussion (Docherty & Smith 1999) | Consistent. Open with principal findings; implications per actor. |
| Conclusion | Conclusive; limitations → recommendations; further research | Limitations tied to inference; conclusion is not a summary | Consistent. Each limitation names the inference affected. |
| Outline, then write | After the findings gate | Outline first; time on title, abstract, figures, outline (Mensh & Kording 2017; Belcher 2019) | Consistent. Add a reverse outline before submission. |
